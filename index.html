When Seeing Overrides Knowing

Visual Dominance and Deferral-Based Method for Personalized Safety in VLMs

Ask a vision-language model whether the dish in a photo is fine for lunch and it will usually say yes. That answer works for most people and could send someone with a nut allergy to the hospital. The model cannot see who is asking, so a safe model has to notice when the missing context matters and ask before it answers. We call this personalized safety and study it in models that see images as well as text.

MPS-Bench

MPS-Bench pairs 584 real user images with questions across 12 high-risk domains, from health and finance to caregiving. Every scenario comes with a hidden user profile covering things like age, health, emotional state and substance use. The model only sees the image and the question. The judge sees the full profile and scores the answer on five safety dimensions. We also vary the tone of the image and of the text, which lets us see which one the model actually listens to.

VLMs rarely ask for context

When important context is missing, the safe move is often a quick follow-up question. Models almost never do this. They answer directly 86 to 99 percent of the time, and models that reason before answering are no more careful.

Direct answers are unsafe

Those direct answers score poorly. Without the user profile, no model gets above 2.6 out of 5 in any domain. Give the same models the profile and every one of them does much better. The ability is there. What the models lack is the sense that something important is missing.

Visual dominance

Changing the tone of the image moves safety scores far more than changing the tone of the question. A grim photo makes models cautious however the question is worded, while a pleasant photo can make a risky question look harmless. We call this visual dominance, and it holds for every model we tested.

Where it happens inside the model

To find the source, we feed Gemma-3 4B and 12B 2,000 paired inputs that share the same question and differ only in the image, then patch individual attention edges. The image effect reaches the final decision mostly through the text tokens, carrying 2.1 to 2.3 times more of it than the image tokens do. The picture gets written into the text early in the network and read back out near the end. Because the mixing starts so early, patching late layers cannot cleanly undo it.

PRISM knows when to defer

Since the model cannot reliably fix this on its own, PRISM checks the input before the model answers. It is a small module (10 to 20M trainable parameters) on top of frozen image and text encoders. Each modality reweights the features of the other, so risk cues in the text are not drowned out by the image. When PRISM decides a question needs more context, the assistant asks the user first.

PRISM reaches 0.95 to 0.98 AUROC across four encoders, well above VLM judges (0.76 to 0.82) and linear probes (0.67 to 0.79). It also gives the lowest unsafe exposure at every deferral rate, so it catches risky questions without deferring on everything.

Abstract

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes settings, where a response that is reasonable in general may still be unsafe for a particular user whose medical, emotional, or situational context is unknown to the model. We study this problem of personalized safety in multimodal systems and introduce MPS-Bench, a benchmark of 5,181 scenarios from 584 real-world images across 12 high-risk domains, each paired with a hidden user profile. Evaluating eight frontier VLMs, we find that they almost always respond directly (86–99%) rather than seek missing context, and none exceeds 2.6/5 on personalized safety. To understand why these failures arise, we analyze multimodal interactions and identify visual dominance, where visual information enters text representations early and suppresses textual risk signals during multimodal fusion. Causal interventions reveal a two-stage mechanism in which visual affect is first transferred into the text stream in early layers and then shapes the final decision through this altered text representation, making late-stage internal remediation unreliable. Motivated by this mechanism, we propose PRISM, a lightweight input monitor that uses bidirectional cross-modal modulation to predict when a query is likely to require deferral. PRISM achieves 0.978 AUC and strictly dominates the safety–utility Pareto frontier across all tested models.
